title: Parallel effects
description: "fx.on_thread runs an effect as a run of its own on another thread: on free-threaded Python, CPU-heavy effects use several cores. What it costs and when it pays."
---

Every fiber of a run takes turns on the run's thread (see [Threads and throughput](/docs/threads)),
so two CPU-heavy fibers of one run never compute at the same time. `fx.on_thread(eff)` runs `eff`
as the root fiber of a run of its own, on a new thread, and takes on its result: its value, its
typed failure or its defect. Effects under `on_thread` go in parallel with the caller and with
each other on free-threaded Python (3.14t). On the GIL build they still take turns, at bytecode
boundaries instead of fiber steps, so the caller's run keeps serving its other fibers while one
computes, as with `fx.blocking`.

The effect sees the caller's services. Interrupting the caller interrupts the effect on its own
run and waits for its finalizers, as interrupting a forked fiber does, so a timeout or a race
around `on_thread` cleans up what the effect opened. What the effect forks and leaves running
goes on in the background once it ends, as after `fx.run_blocking`.

## `fx.blocking`, `fx.on_thread` or a process

- A plain function, however heavy: `fx.blocking(f)`. It already runs on a pool thread, in
  parallel on 3.14t, and costs less to start. Interrupting it abandons the thread.
- An effect of many steps (a loop of `fx.sync` calls, a stream, a parser written with
  effects): `fx.on_thread(eff)`. It can be interrupted, and its finalizers run.
- Parallel work on the GIL build: a `ProcessWorker` from [workers](/docs/workers), or one
  process per core.

## What it measured

[`benchmarks/parallel.py`](https://github.com/tylergibbs1/pyfect/blob/main/benchmarks/parallel.py)
runs N copies of the same work with `concurrency=N`. "Pure" is one pure-Python function (about
100 ms); "steps" is an effect of 100,000 `fx.sync` steps. The rate is N times one task's time
alone over the fan-out's time: 1.0x is no faster than one at a time. Best of three, on an
18-core Apple M-series machine that other jobs were also using, so read the ratios, not the
milliseconds:

| Work | N | 3.14 inline | 3.14 `on_thread` | 3.14t inline | 3.14t `blocking` | 3.14t `on_thread` |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| pure | 4 | 1.04x | 0.97x | 1.01x | 2.87x | 3.38x |
| pure | 8 | 1.27x | 1.15x | 0.97x | 4.52x | 4.20x |
| steps | 4 | 1.04x | 1.05x | 1.09x | n/a | 1.58x |
| steps | 8 | 0.97x | 0.92x | 1.11x | n/a | 2.57x |

On the GIL build nothing runs faster, and `on_thread` costs a few percent. On 3.14t a pure
function scales the same through either. An effect of many steps scales, but less than a
pure function: every step touches objects all threads share (the runtime's classes, methods
and constants), and free-threaded CPython keeps their reference counts with atomic operations
that the cores fight over. A plain thread calling `fx.run_blocking` scales no better.

Starting and finishing a hand-off, from asking to the work starting and from the work ending to
the caller running again, p50 / p95 in milliseconds:

| | 3.14 unloaded | 3.14t unloaded | 3.14t, a busy thread per core |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `blocking` start | 0.03 / 0.07 | 0.01 / 0.04 | 0.03 / 0.13 |
| `blocking` finish | 0.03 / 0.07 | 0.01 / 0.06 | 0.07 / 1.2 |
| `on_thread` start | 0.05 / 0.12 | 0.07 / 0.14 | 0.08 / 0.14 |
| `on_thread` finish | 0.06 / 0.14 | 0.03 / 0.09 | 0.25 / 1.0 |

`on_thread` starts a thread per call, so it suits work of a millisecond or more. With every core
busy, a wake-up waits for the OS to give the waiting thread a core: on 3.14t the finish's p95
went from about 1 ms (the table, N=4) to 15 ms for `on_thread` and 25 ms for `blocking` (a
run with N=8), while on the GIL build either waits for the GIL behind every busy thread, with
a p50 of 30 to 130 ms.
